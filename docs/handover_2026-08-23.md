### GW2 can now be decided at all

Before this weekend it could not. `live/recommendation.py` supports only gameweek one and

- **#178 — what a capture's counters describe.** The prerequisite nobody had measured. In a
  capture taken before the season's first kick-off, **454 of 461 players** carry their *previous*
  season's `total_points` and `minutes` **exactly**; after the first kick-off the platform resets
  and **404 of 461** players' accumulated minutes *fall*, which a counter that only accumulates
  cannot do. So the meaning of those fields flips at one instant and nothing in the payload says
  which side you are on. `in_season_totals` now derives the phase from the capture's own two
  payloads and refuses to read the counters when it is wrong. There are **three** phases, not
  two: the reset happens somewhere in the ninety minutes between the opening deadline and the
  first kick-off, no capture exists inside that window, so that window refuses rather than
  guesses. Record: `docs/capture_season_phase.md`.

- **#182 — the in-season blend.** The opening control told about the season so far. The two
  stages are blended **separately**, because blending finished points collapses algebraically to
  season-to-date points per gameweek and throws the minutes away — it could then not tell ninety
  minutes from twenty for the same return. Each stage is shrunk by its own sample in its own
  unit: playing time by gameweeks elapsed (prior worth 6, the control's `rate_window`), the
  scoring rate by **minutes** (prior worth 270, `CrossSeasonConfig.min_minutes`).

  Worth knowing because it was a real defect caught by running against the capture rather than
  by fixtures: the first version took a lone in-season observation at full weight, and Christos
  Tzolis — 75 minutes, 6 points, no prior Premier League record — came out at exactly **6.0
  expected points, above Haaland**, from one appearance. Such a player now shrinks on the points
  scale toward the same opening-price prior the opening control gives him, and lands at 2.83.

- **#183 — the producer.** `scripts/build_projection_handoff.py`. Season and target gameweek are
  **derived from the capture**, not passed, because the live path compares season, gameweek *and*
  capture before it will use a handoff. `handoff_path_for` and `write_projection_handoff` are
  imported rather than reimplemented, and the script reads its own output back through
  `read_projection_handoff` and compares fingerprints, so producer/consumer agreement is measured
  on every run. Verified on a real capture: 600 of 600 players priced, written to
  `data/handoffs/2026-27-gw02.json`, read back identically.

- **#186 — the runbook names the command.** The GW2-onward block ran a capture and then a decide

### The GW2 model is measured, and honestly

- **#193 / #195 — the walk-forward benchmark.** 147 development folds, nine projections on
  identical folds under one fixed optimizer. The comparison is deliberately *not* the blend
  against itself: it is scored against the archive-fed control above it and against ignoring the
  season entirely below it.

  **Reading the season so far is worth +13.78 [11.68, 16.86], 4 of 4 seasons positive.** That is
  the cleanest number — the two configurations differ only in whether the in-season term exists.

  **The weight axis does not resolve at this precision, and #195 is why.** The differences it
  asks about are 0.16 to 0.84 points; re-running one configuration moves it by up to 0.22,
  because the lexicographic tie-break that chooses between equal-objective squads does not finish
  on every fold. Three of nine configurations did not reproduce, and the two largest moves belong
  to the two least determined. So "the declared weights sit on top of a flat surface" now rests
  on the measurement being unable to separate them — not on their having been separated and won.
  Same practical answer (leave them alone), honest reason.

  Caveat in the record rather than a footnote: `FITTED_OPENING_PRICE_COEFFICIENT` was fitted on
  2020-21 through 2024-25, the same seasons these folds evaluate, so absolute levels are
  optimistic for all nine configurations and a control-versus-blend gap could partly reflect
  differing reliance on that constant. Quantifying that exposure is named as the first follow-up.

- **#196 — the model's own residual history.** 101,447 rows over the same 147 folds, residual
  mean −0.3783, sd 2.2171. #45 asks for the spread a live recommendation carries and states the
  rule that makes such a number honest: the residuals must belong to the model that is deciding.
  A control residual export already existed — worth saying, because it nearly got rebuilt — but
  it carries `model_version = form_window_05_v1`, the archive-fed control, and from GW2 the
  decision is made by `in-season-carry-over-v1`. Opening gameweeks are **refused**, not filtered:
  this history cannot describe a carry-over-and-price-prior projection, and pretending otherwise
  is how a confident-looking interval gets printed with nothing behind it.

  It is an **input**, not a calibration claim. Whether these residuals produce honest coverage is
  a separate measurement.

## What "the formal run" actually requires

Asked whether the Route A run could just be fired. It cannot — measured, not assumed:

1. `scripts/run_candidate_gate.py` is **hardwired to #43**: it imports
   `freeze_candidate_declaration.benchmark_config`, its frozen-record path is
   `docs/issue43_candidate_declaration.json`, and its outputs are named `issue43_*`.
2. `LearnedRateConfig` has only `window`, `ridge_alpha`, `min_training_rows`; `input_columns` is
   **derived from the window** and cannot take extra columns. Adding the two signals is a change
   to `prediction/learned_rate.py`.
3. **Nothing joins the signal frame onto a feature frame** in the candidate path.
   `experiments/opponent_projection.py` references the columns, but that is the S2 study.

So the run is roughly a day: extend the rate config to accept declared extra inputs, build the
candidate projection builder that joins the signal per fold, add a Route-A entry point, tests,
then 1.5–2.5 hours of compute. **The safe split is: the runner is ordinary reviewable code; the
run is a once-only act** ("evaluated once", per the declaration). Building the runner first and
firing separately keeps the code reviewed and the measurement a deliberate moment.

### #192 in particular

There is a measured comment on the thread. Short version: **the lexicographic tie-break already
exists and you wrote it** (#47, 2026-08-14, "make production benchmark solving reproducible"),
and it is stronger than the fix the issue proposes — it orders captain, then starting XI, then
squad, so it disambiguates the bench too.

The defect is not a missing tie-break but a **silently skipped** one. It runs only when the
primary solve is OPTIMAL, more than 1 ms of wall clock remains, and deterministic budget is
available; fail any one and `tiebreak_attempted` stays false and the squad falls to search
order. Measured on 30 development folds under plain `EvaluationConfig()`: primary 30/30 OPTIMAL,
tie-break 30/30 attempted, **28/30 completed**.

So the first diagnostic step is cheap and the data already exists: compare `tiebreak_attempted`
and `tiebreak_completed` between the recorded GW1 ledger entry and the replay. If they differ,
this is a budget/configuration question, not a new solve. **The published view cannot answer it** —
`recommendation.json` carries `solver_proved_optimal` and `solver_status` but no `tiebreak_*`
field, so the replay guarantee is not auditable from the site. Two fields would fix that.

Second observation on the same thread, possibly its own issue: with
`DEFAULT_BENCHMARK_DETERMINISTIC_TIME_LIMIT = 0.5`, **16 of 30 folds never prove optimality**,
the tie-break completes once in thirty, and the budget is exhausted on 29 of 30. The guard at
`production_benchmark.py:535-548` is correctly written — it flags an incomplete solve *unless*
the budget was exhausted — but with this budget the exhaustion condition is nearly always true,
so it excuses almost every incomplete solve instead of catching one. The control's tables solve
to optimality at that budget and the blend's do not, which is a property of the blend (many
players priced from the same prior, so a large set of squads shares the top objective) and is
recorded in the blend's own benchmark rather than billed to the solver.
